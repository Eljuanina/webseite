### Key takeaways

**Why experiments?** Prof. Kenneth Gillingham explained that comparing outcomes before and after a policy, or across regions, often suffers from selection and confounding. Randomisation solves this at the source and prevents programmes from being scaled up on the basis of overestimated effects.

**Solar and carbon pricing.** Experiments on residential solar showed that information and social-norm campaigns can trigger contagion between neighbours. Self-interest messages worked better in affluent areas, pro-social messages in lower-income ones. In an RCT on internal carbon pricing at Yale, an actual price had a clearly larger effect than information alone.

**Innovation policy.** Albert Bravo-Biosca pointed out that European governments spend around 150 billion euros a year on innovation support, yet very few programmes are evaluated against a credible control group. Randomised trials, for example with innovation vouchers or competitive funding calls, can show what works and help scale it.

**Limits.** Results from one setting do not always carry over to others, and not every policy can be randomised, carbon pricing for the whole economy being one example. A good first step for institutions is a small fund dedicated to trials, signalling that experimentation is welcome.
